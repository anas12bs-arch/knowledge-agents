> Source: legal | Category: legal | 2026-11-09T19:45:18Z

U.S. Supreme Court Deals Final Blow to Corporate Human Rights Accountability

For the past two decades, the U.S. Supreme Court has been slowly  weakening the Alien Tort Statute (ATS) , a key human rights law that allows foreign nationals to sue persons or entities in civil court for harms resulting from violations of  international law . The court’s  recent ruling  in   Cisco Systems, Inc. v. Doe I   dealt the law a final blow, foreclosing—absent new legislation by Congress—any future ability by plaintiffs to use the law to hold technology companies accountable for aiding and abetting human rights abuses. EFF  filed multiple amicus briefs in the case , including most recently in the Supreme Court. 
  The Supreme Court Rejected New Causes of Action Under the ATS  
 The ATS was passed by the very first Congress in 1789 and in  just a few words  gives U.S. federal courts jurisdiction to hear civil claims brought by foreign nationals against defendants who violated “the law of nations or a treaty of the United States." There has been a longstanding question about what counts as a valid civil claim under “the law of nations.” 
 In   Sosa v. Alvarez-Machain   (2004), the Supreme Court had noted three historical international law claims that the First Congress likely had in mind: violation of  safe conducts , infringement of the rights of ambassadors, and piracy, collectively known as the  Blackstone Three . 
 The  Sosa  court also held that federal courts may recognize  other  substantive causes of action under the ATS if they reflect international norms that are “specific, universal, and obligatory,” stating that this “door is still ajar subject to vigilant doorkeeping, and thus open to a narrow class of international norms today.” 
 Thus, the  Sosa  court recognized that the ATS could be used to bring claims related to historical violations of international law as well as select newer claims reflecting modern concepts of human rights.
