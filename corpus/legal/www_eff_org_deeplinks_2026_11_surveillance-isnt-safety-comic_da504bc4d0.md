# [eff] Surveillance Isn't Safety: A Comic About Organizing Against Flock and Other Foes

> Source: legal | Category: legal | 2026-11-18T16:26:05Z

Surveillance Isn't Safety: A Comic About Organizing Against Flock and Other Foes

In the new comic "Surveillance Isn't Safety"—available for free today— cartoonist Bryn Adams explores the growing movement against mass surveillance through their involvement in the community fight against Flock Safety in Cleveland, Ohio. It's a personal journey that will resonate widely with residents in hundreds of cities where communities are drawing the line against    automated license plate readers    (ALPRs).

  Published in collaboration between EFF and    Crucial Comix   , Bryn's comic captures the authenticity of the grassroot backlash against the surveillance industry—undercutting Flock founder Garrett Langley's    latest dubious defense    that public opposition of his company's product isn't real.  
  At the International Association of Chiefs of Police conference this fall, Langley accused the anti-surveillance activism of being driven by Russian bots and as being "inorganic." He pointed to the fact that many local groups were using similar language in their protest signs as supposed proof, when in reality, shared slogans are one of the hallmarks of a social movement. This was all misdirection from the grim realities that Flock and other ALPR systems facilitate: the    mistakes    are real; the    abuse    is real; the    racism    is real; and the targeting of    dissenters   ,    migrants   , and    abortion seekers    is real. And, importantly,    the cancelled contracts    are real.   
  Bryn's comic sheds light on the real abuses and threats driving communities to mobilize against Flock, Axon, and other ALPR vendors. It shows that coast-to-coast activism doesn't happen overnight—any more than a comic appears out of thin air as a finished product. As advisors on the cartoon, EFF watched how Bryn's script evolved from rough sketches to penciled drawings, then to inked and shaded panels, mirroring how the long-running movement against mass surveillance has steadily snowballed over the last few year
