# [behavioral-scientist] Frontiers: A Roundtable Discussion on the I-Frame vs. S-Frame Debate

> Source: psychology | Category: psychology | 2026-10-06T23:23:27Z

Frontiers: A Roundtable Discussion on the I-Frame vs. S-Frame Debate

Applied behavioral scientists believe their work can influence people’s lives for the better. But what is the most effective way to make that happen?&nbsp; 
   
   
 Is it through interventions focused on influencing individual behavior while maintaining freedom of choice? Is it by changing policies and laws, and thereby the systems, that individuals operate within? Is it a combination of the two? Is the distinction between an individual- or systems-focus a helpful one? Or, is the world too messy and complex? How should applied behavioral scientists navigate the financial and political influences on the work they’re asked to do, as well as the work they’d like to do?  
   
   
 These are all questions that applied behavioral scientists are grappling with as they try to realize the promise of the field—to leverage the scientific understanding of psychology, behavior, society, and culture to help one another live happier, healthier, and more peaceful lives.  
   
   
 On October 14, join Editor-in-Chief Evan Nesterak and five leading behavioral scientists—Nick Chater, George Loewestein, Crystal Hall, Michael Hallsworth, and Chiara Varazzani—for a roundtable discussion about these questions and more. The leaping off point will be the i-frame vs. s-frame debate initiated by Chater and Loewenstein, first in an  academic article  and now in their new book,   It’s on You   .  (Further background on the debate below.) 
   
   
 The guests of the roundtable don’t all agree on how to answer the questions above. In fact, in many cases they sharply disagree. But they all have extensive experience working in both academic and applied settings, especially in public policy, so we have the opportunity for an interesting and constructive conversation. The goal isn’t to settle the debate but to explore areas of agreement and disagreement, open a dialogue about how applied behavioral scientists approach their work, and surface the questions that can move the debate forward.
